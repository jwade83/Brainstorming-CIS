§ 01

What Is Hauntology?

What the idea is testing: Why our political, cultural, and technological present feels “haunted” — inhabited by things that are neither fully present nor fully absent.

Quick answer

  • Hauntology was coined by Jacques Derrida in Spectres of Marx (1993).
  • It’s a pun: in French, hantologie sounds almost identical to ontologie (“ontology,” the study of being).
  • The joke has a point: what haunts — the spectre, the not-quite-present — unsettles our neat categories of “is” and “is not,” present and absent, living and dead.
  • Derrida wrote it after the Soviet collapse, against the triumphant “end of history” mood of the early 1990s.
  • The claim: our systems are haunted by alternatives that never came to be — futures foreclosed, roads not taken.

Deeper detail

The historical moment. By 1993 the Berlin Wall had fallen and the USSR had dissolved. Francis Fukuyama had declared The End of History — the argument that liberal capitalism had won for good and no serious alternative remained. Derrida’s Spectres of Marx is largely a rebuttal: you cannot declare a rival dead and expect it to stay buried. Marx’s ghost keeps returning to haunt the very system that pronounced him finished.

Why “spectre,” not just “memory.” A spectre is structurally strange. It is not present (it’s dead, gone, unrealised) but not absent either (it acts on us, shapes us, demands something). Hauntology names exactly this in-between status — being “out of joint,” as Hamlet says of his own haunted time. It is the study of things that persist as absence: the influence of what didn’t happen.

On the video’s framing. The clip says hauntology means our systems are “haunted by the alternative ideologies that never came to be.” That’s a fair one-line gloss, but Derrida’s target was sharper: the specific ghost of Marx haunting a capitalism that believed it had won. The generalisation to “alternative ideologies” is really Fisher’s later expansion, not Derrida’s original emphasis.

Threads worth following

  • Why is Hamlet (“the time is out of joint”) Derrida’s touchstone text for hauntology?
  • How does hauntology relate to Derrida’s older idea of the trace — presence always shadowed by what it excludes?
  • What was Fukuyama’s End of History actually arguing, and why did Derrida treat it as the thing to refute?
§ 02

Fisher’s Turn: What Haunts the 21st Century?

What this is testing: How a piece of 1990s political philosophy got repurposed to diagnose contemporary culture.

Quick answer

  • Mark Fisher (1968–2017), British theorist and critic, took Derrida’s term and asked a new question: what haunts us now?
  • His answer: not ideologies, people, or the past — but the futures we once imagined and never got.
  • We are haunted by our own cancelled futures: the tomorrows 20th-century culture promised and the 21st failed to deliver.
  • Key works: Capitalist Realism (2009) and Ghosts of My Life (2014).

Deeper detail

From politics to culture. Where Derrida did political philosophy, Fisher was a cultural critic — writing about music, film, and design. He noticed that late-20th and early-21st-century culture had a strange relationship to time: it kept recycling its own past instead of producing anything genuinely new. Vintage aesthetics, endless reissues, sampling, retro revivals. Culture stopped feeling like it was moving forward.

The specific haunting. For Fisher the ghost isn’t the past as such — nostalgia already covers that. The ghost is the lost future: the sleek, liberated, collectively imagined tomorrow that mid-century modernity promised and that simply didn’t arrive. What haunts us is the gap between the future we were promised and the recycled present we inhabit.

“The death of the imagination for alternative ways of being.” This ties to Fisher’s other big idea, capitalist realism: the sense that capitalism is the only viable system, that “it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism.” Hauntology is the mood of capitalist realism — the melancholy of a culture that can no longer picture a different way of living, only remixes of what already exists.

Threads worth following

  • What is capitalist realism, and how does it explain the “there is no alternative” feeling?
  • Fisher wrote extensively about music (Burial, dubstep, the Ghost Box label). Why did sound become his central evidence?
  • Fisher’s own struggles with depression shaped Ghosts of My Life. How does he link the personal and the political?
§ 03

Nostalgia vs. Hauntology

What this is testing: The most common misreading — collapsing hauntology into “missing the good old days.”

Quick answer

  • Nostalgia longs for the past — a real (or idealised) time that did exist.
  • Hauntology longs for a future imagined in the past that never came to be — a time that never existed.
  • Nostalgia wants to go back. Hauntology mourns a forward that was cancelled.

Deeper detail

The distinction is the whole game. Nostalgia is comfortable and consumable: it repackages the 1980s, the 1950s, a childhood, and sells it back to you. It points backward at something that happened.

Hauntology points at an absence in the future tense. It’s the ache of 1960s Space-Age imagery — not because you want to live in the 1960s, but because that era’s idea of the year 2000 (moon bases, transformed society) was so much bolder than the actual 2000s turned out to be. You aren’t missing the past; you’re missing the future the past believed in.

This is why hauntological art often sounds like decayed or half-remembered media — crackle, tape hiss, faded VHS. It’s the aesthetic of a signal from a future that got lost in transmission. Nostalgia restores the old image cleanly; hauntology keeps the damage, because the damage is the point.

Threads worth following

  • Why does “retro” marketing sell so easily, while hauntology stays melancholy and hard to commodify?
  • Is nostalgia a symptom of the same cultural stall Fisher describes, or its opposite?
§ 04

The Slow Cancellation of the Future

What this is testing: Fisher’s core diagnosis of why new futures stopped arriving.

Quick answer

  • After the late 20th century, technology accelerated but cultural imagination narrowed.
  • Instead of new imagined futures, culture began recycling old narratives, infrastructures, and expectations.
  • Fisher — borrowing a phrase from Franco “Bifo” Berardi — calls this the “slow cancellation of the future.”

Deeper detail

The paradox at the centre. The gadgets kept improving — faster processors, smartphones, streaming. But culturally, things flattened. Compare the aesthetic distance between 1960 and 1980 (enormous — whole new genres and looks) to the distance between 2005 and 2025 (comparatively small). Technology sprinted; imagination idled.

Why “slow” and why “cancellation.” No single event killed the future. It’s a gradual attrition — a reboot here, a legacy sequel there, a remaster — until you look up and the culture is feeding on itself. The future wasn’t destroyed; it was quietly cancelled, the way a TV show gets cancelled: the sets still stand, but no new episodes are made.

Attribution. “Slow cancellation of the future” is often credited to Fisher, and he popularised it, but he took it from the Italian theorist Franco “Bifo” Berardi. Worth crediting both — the video attributes it to Fisher alone.

Remakes and reboots as evidence. The unease at yet another franchise reboot, live-action remake, or “requel” isn’t just taste. It’s a small, felt instance of the slow cancellation: culture demonstrating, in real time, that it would rather re-run a known quantity than risk imagining something new.

Threads worth following

  • Is the “stall” real, or a selection effect (we remember past innovations and forget the filler)?
  • How do economics — risk-averse studios, franchise IP, algorithmic recommendation — materially drive the recycling?
§ 05 · Case Study

The Backrooms — Empty Promises

What this is testing: How an internet horror myth stages hauntology as empty space.

Quick answer

  • The Backrooms is a liminal-space myth: endless empty corporate interiors — yellowed wallpaper, buzzing fluorescent lights, familiar office and retail architecture, devastatingly empty.
  • Through Fisher’s lens: these symbolise the infrastructures of 21st-century life — corporate prosperity, stable careers, endless growth, modern efficiency.
  • The horror: we still live inside these infrastructures, but the futures they promised never arrived. The spaces remain; the promise is gone.

Deeper detail

Origin. The Backrooms began around 2019 as a single unsettling photo and caption on 4chan, snowballing into a vast collaborative mythology (later a much-shared found-footage series by Kane Pixels). The defining trait is the liminal space: architecture built for transit and commerce — hallways, waiting rooms, office floors, mall corridors — encountered empty, at the wrong hour, drained of the people and purpose that were supposed to fill it.

Why it’s hauntological, not just creepy. These aren’t ruins or haunted houses. They’re the ordinary environments of late capitalism — the office, the retail floor, the corporate campus. Each was built as a machine for delivering a future: the career, the growth, the prosperity. The Backrooms show that machinery still standing and still lit, but hollow — the human future it was meant to produce has evaporated. That is exactly Fisher’s ghost: the promise persisting as an empty shell you’re still trapped inside.

“The spaces remain, but the futures that they promised are devastatingly empty.”

Threads worth following

  • Why do liminal spaces (thresholds, in-between places) carry this charge? Connect to the anthropology of liminality (Victor Turner).
  • Fisher’s The Weird and the Eerie (2016) gives vocabulary: the eerie is precisely about absence and agency — “why is there nothing here when there should be something?”
§ 06 · Case Study

The Farlands — Broken Edges

What this is testing: A second, contrasting face of the same haunting — not emptiness, but the edge of the imaginable.

Quick answer

  • The Farlands originated in Minecraft: push far enough toward the edges of the generated world and the terrain breaks — walls of distorted geometry where generation fails.
  • The metaphor migrated to TikTok: pushing your algorithm toward its strangest, most niche edges until the recommendation engine’s “rules begin to break.”
  • Through Fisher’s lens: not empty like the Backrooms but broken. The horror is the death of the imagination: worlds that felt infinite turn out to have terrifyingly finite edges.

Deeper detail

Origin (the real bug). In early Minecraft, world generation relied on floating-point math that lost precision at extreme coordinates (around 12.5 million blocks from spawn). The result was the “Far Lands”: a chaotic wall of malformed terrain marking the literal edge of the computable world. A bug, but a poetic one — proof that the “infinite” sandbox had a boundary where its own logic disintegrated.

The TikTok translation. To reach “the Farlands” of TikTok is to push the recommendation algorithm — through niche, recursive, increasingly strange engagement — until the feed stops feeling curated and starts feeling malfunctioning, surfacing content from the weird outer edge of the system. Same shape as the Minecraft bug: keep going toward the boundary and the generating system breaks down.

Why it’s hauntological. The Backrooms haunt through empty promise; the Farlands through broken infinity. Our algorithmic worlds — the feed, the platform, the endless scroll — present themselves as boundless. The Farlands reveal the lie: there is an edge, and at the edge the machinery just produces glitched repetitions of itself. That’s the “death of the imagination” made literal — the infinite generator running out of genuinely new things to generate, and breaking.

Threads worth following

  • Backrooms = the eerie of absence (nothing where there should be something); Farlands = the eerie of malfunction (the system failing at its own edge). Two different Fisherian moods?
  • How does the “infinite feed” with a hidden finite edge connect back to the slow cancellation — abundance of content, scarcity of the genuinely new?
§ 07 · Synthesis

Liminal Horror as Our Cultural Condition

What this is testing: Pulling the strands together into a single claim.

Quick answer

  • For Fisher, hauntology is living among the ghosts of futures that never happened.
  • The Backrooms (empty) and the Farlands (broken) both depict worlds where past expectations of the future and current lived reality are fractured.
  • We can’t metabolise the difference between what was promised and what is — so we’re trapped in an echo of everything we could have been.

Deeper detail

The two myths are complementary diagnoses of one condition:

Backrooms vs. Farlands
The BackroomsThe Farlands
Core imageEmpty corporate interiorsBroken edges of a generated world
Failure modeThe promise is hollowThe infinity is finite
Haunted byFutures promised but never filledThe end of the imagination’s frontier
Fisher’s moodThe eerie of absenceThe eerie of malfunction
Everyday echoCareers, growth, “prosperity” that didn’t materialiseFeeds that seemed endless, then hit their limit

Both are liminal: thresholds, in-between zones, edges. That’s the point of calling liminal horror our current cultural condition. We are, collectively, standing in a doorway — inside infrastructures built for a future that never came, scrolling worlds that promised infinity and delivered a loop. The horror isn’t a monster. It’s the quiet recognition, in a fluorescent-lit corridor or at the glitched edge of a feed, that the future got cancelled while we were still living in its architecture.

Threads worth following

  • Is liminal horror a genuinely new structure of feeling, or the latest costume for older anxieties (the uncanny, the sublime, fin-de-siècle dread)?
  • If the imagination has stalled, what would un-cancelling the future look like? (Fisher’s later “acid communism” fragment gestures at this.)
  • Counter-argument: is the “lost future” thesis itself a kind of nostalgia — mourning a modernism that was never as universal as it’s remembered?
§ 08 · Media Lineage

How the Domain Has Progressed

What this is testing: Hauntology and liminal horror aren’t just a theory — they’re a living aesthetic that has moved through media over ~25 years, mutating each time it changed platforms. From academic philosophy to folk-internet genre to its own self-aware discourse (the explainer that started these notes).

Quick answer

  • 2000s — Music. Hauntology is first an art movement, mostly electronic music. The material Fisher actually wrote about.
  • ~2010 — Internet aesthetics. Vaporwave translates the mood into a native-online meme about dead consumer futures.
  • Mid–late 2010s — Analog horror (YouTube). Degraded-broadcast storytelling turns the aesthetic of decayed media into a horror grammar.
  • 2020s — Liminal-space myths & games. The Backrooms, the Farlands, and walking-sim games make the space itself the subject.
  • 2020s–now — Short-form video essays. Creators like @Eterna hand the whole lineage back as theory — the domain becomes self-aware.

Each stage doesn’t replace the last; it layers on top, and the tools get more accessible each time (studio → bedroom producer → YouTube channel → game engine → phone).

Stage 1 · Music — the original canon (~1998–2011)

The ground truth, because it’s the media Fisher himself analysed.

  • Burial — Untrue (2007). Crackle, surface noise, and ghostly pitched vocals that sound like a rave remembered rather than attended — the lost collective euphoria of ’90s UK dance music.
  • Ghost Box (label, 2004) — Belbury Poly, The Focus Group, Pye Corner Audio. A “parallel England” of library music and public-information films, mourning the abandoned postwar social-democratic future.
  • The Caretaker (Leyland Kirby) — Everywhere at the End of Time (2016–2019) musically enacts dementia through decaying ballroom loops. Notably, this pre-internet-native work went massively viral on TikTok around 2020 — the lineage folding back on itself.
  • William Basinski — The Disintegration Loops (2002): tape literally crumbling as it plays, recorded against the backdrop of 9/11. Decay as content.
  • Boards of Canada — Music Has the Right to Children (1998): warped, sun-faded analog nostalgia that prefigures the whole mood.

Stage 2 · Vaporwave — the internet-native turn (~2010–2015)

Hauntology leaves the record label and becomes a meme anyone with a laptop can make. Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1 (2010) and Macintosh Plus — Floral Shoppe (2011) are the touchstones: slowed, chopped ’80s–’90s corporate pop and muzak, wrapped in Greco-Roman busts and dead-mall imagery. The point is explicitly hauntological — it mourns the utopia consumer capitalism promised by exhuming its muzak and letting it rot. Subgenres like mallsoft make the “devastatingly empty commercial interior” into a sound years before it was a horror image.

Stage 3 · Analog horror on YouTube (~2015–now)

The decayed-media aesthetic becomes a full storytelling grammar. Local58 (2015), Gemini Home Entertainment (2019), The Mandela Catalogue (2021), The Walten Files (2020); the ARG Petscop (2017). The shared move: tell horror through the format of obsolete media — VHS tracking errors, emergency broadcasts, corrupted PSAs, glitched PS1 games. The medium is the haunting: a signal from a past that imagined a safe, ordered future, returning corrupted.

Stage 4 · Liminal-space myths & games (2019–now)

Now the space itself is the subject — the stage these notes’ two case studies belong to. Folklore: the Backrooms (2019 →, supercharged by Kane Pixels’ 2022 found-footage series, an A24 film in development) and the Farlands. Games make it interactive: The Exit 8 (2023 — an endless looping subway passage you escape only by noticing anomalies), Pools (2024), The Complex: Found Footage (2022); adjacent AAA, Control (2019) and its brutalist “Oldest House.” Screen work runs in parallel: Skinamarink (2022), Twin Peaks: The Return (2017), Adam Curtis’s HyperNormalisation (2016).

Stage 5 · The video-essay meta-turn (2020s–now)

The final progression is reflexive. Creators on TikTok and YouTube — the @Eterna clip these notes come from is a textbook example — take the whole 25-year lineage and hand it back to the audience as theory, in 60–180 seconds, on the very platforms the theory is now about. The domain has become self-aware: the medium diagnosing the condition is itself one of the “algorithmic worlds with terrifying finite edges” it describes.

Threads worth following

  • Accessibility as the driver. Each stage lowers the barrier to production (label → DAW → screen-capture → game engine → phone). Does democratised tooling spread hauntology, or dilute it into content?
  • The recursion problem. When the critique of “recycled culture” becomes an algorithmically-rewarded content genre, has it escaped the slow cancellation or become another instance of it?
  • The Caretaker on TikTok is the sharpest case: a 2010s art project about memory-loss going viral as a 2020s challenge. The lineage healing, or eating itself?
  • Where does AI-generated media — infinite, uncanny, edge-less until it suddenly glitches — fit as a possible Stage 6?
Appendix

Glossary

Hauntology
Derrida’s pun on ontology; the study of how the not-quite-present (spectres, lost futures, cancelled alternatives) acts on the present.
Spectre
That which is neither present nor absent, neither living nor dead; the figure at the heart of hauntology.
Capitalist realism (Fisher)
The pervasive sense that capitalism is the only viable system and no alternative can be imagined.
Slow cancellation of the future (Berardi, via Fisher)
The gradual replacement of new cultural production with recycled versions of the past.
Lost / cancelled futures
The tomorrows imagined by past culture that never materialised; hauntology’s primary object of mourning.
The eerie (Fisher)
A mood built on absence or unexplained agency — “why is there nothing here?” / “what is acting here?” (contrast the weird, built on the presence of what shouldn’t be there).
Liminal space
An in-between, transitional environment (corridors, waiting rooms, thresholds), unsettling when encountered empty or out of context.
The Backrooms
Internet horror myth of endless empty liminal interiors (c. 2019–).
The Farlands
The glitched edge of Minecraft’s generated world (a floating-point bug), later a metaphor for pushing an algorithmic feed to its breaking point.
