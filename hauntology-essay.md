# Hauntology: An Articulated Account

*A companion essay to [`hauntology-liminal-horror.md`](./hauntology-liminal-horror.md). Where the notes file is a structured reference, this is a single continuous argument — the concept as developed across our exploration, from Derrida's coinage to the self-aware video essay. Claims are cited with bracketed numbers keyed to the [Sources](#sources) at the end; that bibliography is intended to be exhaustive for everything referenced here.*

---

## 1. The origin — a pun with a point

Hauntology begins as wordplay and ends as a diagnosis of an era. **Jacques Derrida** coined it in *Spectres de Marx* (1993; English *Specters of Marx*, 1994) [1], building it on a near-homophone: in French, *hantologie* ("hauntology") is almost indistinguishable in speech from *ontologie* ("ontology," the philosophy of being). The pun is load-bearing. Ontology asks what *is* — what is present, real, fully itself. Hauntology names everything that troubles that question: the spectre, which is neither present nor absent, neither living nor dead, yet acts on the living all the same [1].

The historical occasion mattered. By 1993 the Berlin Wall had fallen and the Soviet Union had dissolved, and **Francis Fukuyama** had announced *The End of History* — liberal capitalism had won the argument permanently; no serious alternative remained [2]. Derrida's book is in large part a refusal of that triumphalism: you cannot pronounce a rival dead and expect it to stay buried, and Marx's ghost keeps returning to haunt the very order that declared him finished [1]. A spectre, in this sense, is what persists **as absence** — the continuing pressure of something unrealised, foreclosed, or refused. To be haunted is to live in a time that is, in the line from *Hamlet* that Derrida keeps returning to, "out of joint" [1].

The concept also draws on Derrida's older idea of the **trace** — the claim, from *Of Grammatology* (1967), that presence is always shadowed and constituted by what it excludes [3] — and it sits in a long lineage of thought about the unhomely, reaching back to Freud's **uncanny** (*das Unheimliche*, 1919), the familiar made strange by the return of something repressed [4]. The seed of all of it: **the present is never fully itself, because it is inhabited by what did not happen.**

## 2. Fisher's turn — from the ghost of Marx to the ghost of the future

The concept as *we* have used it, though, is really the work of **Mark Fisher** (1968–2017), who took Derrida's political-philosophical term and pointed it at contemporary culture [5][6][7]. The move had been prepared in mid-2000s music criticism — Fisher (writing as *k-punk*) and **Simon Reynolds** developed a musical sense of "hauntology" on their blogs before it reached print [8][9] — but Fisher gave it its mature form in *Ghosts of My Life* (2014) [6] and the essay "What Is Hauntology?" (2012) [10].

Fisher asked a deceptively simple question: if we are haunted now, *what* haunts us? His answer is the pivot of the whole idea. We are **not** primarily haunted by ideologies, by the dead, or by the past as such. We are haunted by **the futures we once imagined and never got** — the lost tomorrows that earlier culture promised and the present quietly failed to deliver [6][10]. The mid-century dream of a transformed society, of radical new art and social forms, of a genuinely different way of living: these didn't merely fail to arrive, they *linger*, as an ache, a sense that we are living in an impoverished version of a future that should have been bolder.

This is where the crucial distinction lives, the one everything else hangs on:

> **Nostalgia longs for the past — a time that *did* exist. Hauntology longs for a future imagined in the past that *never* came to be.**

Nostalgia is comfortable and endlessly saleable; it repackages the eighties or a childhood and sells it back to you, pointing backward at something real. **Fredric Jameson** had already diagnosed this "nostalgia mode" — pastiche, the imitation of dead styles — as a symptom of late capitalism [11], and Fisher's hauntology is the sharper, sadder sibling of that idea. Hauntology points instead at **an absence in the future tense** — not "I miss the 1960s" but "I miss the *year 2000 the 1960s believed in*." This is why genuinely hauntological art tends to *sound and look damaged* — tape hiss, vinyl crackle, faded VHS, decayed loops [6][10]. Nostalgia restores the old image cleanly; hauntology deliberately keeps the corruption, because the damage is the meaning: it is the aesthetic of a signal from a promised future that got lost in transmission.

## 3. The mechanism — the slow cancellation of the future

Fisher's explanation for *why* those futures went missing is his sharpest cultural claim, and he named it — borrowing the phrase from the Italian theorist **Franco "Bifo" Berardi** [12] — the **slow cancellation of the future** [6].

The engine is a paradox. Across the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, *technology accelerated* — faster processors, smartphones, streaming, ubiquitous connectivity — while *cultural imagination narrowed*. Measure the aesthetic distance between 1960 and 1980 (enormous: whole new genres, sounds, and looks) against the distance between 2005 and 2025 (comparatively slight), and the stall becomes visible [6]. The machines sprinted; the imagination idled. Instead of producing new futures, culture began **recycling its own past** — reissues, revivals, remasters, remakes, reboots — a condition **Simon Reynolds** anatomised at book length as *Retromania* (2011) [9].

The word "cancellation" is chosen precisely, and so is "slow." No single event killed the future. It was gradual attrition — a legacy sequel here, a live-action remake there — until you look up and find the culture almost entirely feeding on itself. The future wasn't destroyed; it was *cancelled the way a television show is cancelled*: the sets are still standing, still lit, but no new episodes are being made. This is why the specific unease at yet another franchise reboot isn't merely a matter of taste — it is a small, felt instance of the larger condition.

Underneath sits Fisher's companion concept, **capitalist realism**: the pervasive sense that capitalism is the only viable system, that "it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism" [5]. Hauntology is the *mood* of capitalist realism — the melancholy of a culture that can no longer picture an alternative way of being, only remixes of what already exists. What haunts us, in the end, is **the death of the imagination for alternative futures.**

## 4. Two moods — hauntology and the eerie

As we moved to the internet-myth material, a second distinction became essential — one Fisher draws in *The Weird and the Eerie* (2016) [7]. Alongside hauntology's temporal register (lost futures), there is **the eerie**, a mood built on **absence and unexplained agency**: *why is there nothing here where there should be something? what is acting here that I cannot see?* He distinguishes it from **the weird**, which is the intrusion of something that *should not be present* [7]. These registers — the lost future, the eerie absence, the weird intrusion — are the branches that everything else sorts onto.

The concept also inherits an older anthropological term it leans on constantly: **liminality**, from Arnold van Gennep's *Rites of Passage* (1909) and Victor Turner's elaboration of the "betwixt and between" threshold state [13][14]. A liminal space is transitional by design — a corridor, a waiting room, a doorway — and becomes unsettling when encountered empty or out of its proper use. The phenomenology of such uncanny places has itself been theorised (e.g. Dylan Trigg's *The Memory of Place*, 2012) [15].

## 5. The two internet myths

The two flagship internet myths dramatise the two moods.

**The Backrooms** — originating in a 2019 image-and-caption post on 4chan's /x/ board [16] and given cinematic form by **Kane Pixels'** *The Backrooms (Found Footage)* series (2022) [17] — stage hauntology as *empty space*. Endless liminal corporate interiors — yellowed wallpaper, buzzing fluorescents, familiar office and mall architecture, devastatingly empty. Read through Fisher, these are the *infrastructures of 21st-century life* — the career, the growth, the corporate prosperity, the modern efficiency — shown still standing and still lit, but hollow. We remain trapped inside the machinery built to deliver a future that evaporated. *The spaces remain; the promise is gone.* That is the spectre exactly: the promise persisting as an empty shell. (The kindred SCP entry SCP-3008, the "infinite IKEA," is the same intuition in collaborative-fiction form [18].)

**The Farlands** stage the same haunting as *broken infinity*. The name comes from a real **Minecraft** terrain bug: at roughly 12.5 million blocks from spawn, floating-point imprecision corrupts world generation into a wall of malformed geometry — the literal edge of the computable world, present until it was patched out in the Beta 1.8 update (2011) [19]. Transposed to TikTok, "reaching the Farlands" means pushing the recommendation algorithm through ever more niche, recursive engagement until the feed stops feeling curated and starts feeling *malfunctioning*. Where the Backrooms are empty, the Farlands are **broken**: our algorithmic worlds *present themselves as boundless*, and the horror is the revelation that they have terrifyingly finite edges, where the infinite generator runs out of genuinely new things to generate and begins glitching out repetitions of itself. The death of the imagination, made literal.

Both are **liminal** — thresholds, in-between zones, edges — which is why "liminal horror" names the condition well. We are collectively standing in a doorway: inside infrastructures built for a future that never came, scrolling worlds that promised infinity and delivered a loop. The horror isn't a monster. It is the quiet recognition, in a fluorescent corridor or at the glitched edge of a feed, that the future got cancelled while we were still living in its architecture.

## 6. The domain as a living lineage

Hauntology is not only a theory but a **living aesthetic that has migrated across media for a quarter-century**, mutating each time it changed platforms — and, tellingly, becoming more *accessible* at every step.

- **Music (c. 1998–2011)** is the ground truth, the material Fisher actually analysed: **Boards of Canada's** sun-faded *Music Has the Right to Children* (1998) [20]; **Burial's** *Untrue* (2007), a rave remembered rather than attended [21]; the **Ghost Box** label's "parallel England" of library music and public-information films, mourning an abandoned postwar social-democratic future [22]; **The Caretaker's** *Everywhere at the End of Time* (2016–2019), which enacts dementia through decaying ballroom loops [23]; and **William Basinski's** literally crumbling *Disintegration Loops* (2002–2003) [24].
- **Vaporwave (c. 2010–2015)** took the mood native-online and meme-able — **Chuck Person's** *Eccojams Vol. 1* (2010) [25] and **Macintosh Plus's** *Floral Shoppe* (2011) [26], slowed and chopped corporate muzak over dead-mall imagery — mourning the *utopia consumer capitalism promised*. It made the "empty commercial interior" a *sound* years before the Backrooms made it a horror image.
- **Analog horror on YouTube (c. 2015–now)** — **Local58** (2015) [27], **Petscop** (2017) [28], **Gemini Home Entertainment** (2019) [29], **The Walten Files** (2020) [30], **The Mandela Catalogue** (2021) [31] — turned the *format of obsolete media* (VHS tracking errors, corrupted broadcasts, glitched PS1 games) into a storytelling grammar: the medium itself became the haunting.
- **Liminal-space myths and games (2019–now)** made the space the subject: the Backrooms and Farlands, plus **Control** (2019) and its brutalist "Oldest House" [32], **The Exit 8** (2023) [33], and **Pools** (2024) [34].
- **Short-form video essays (2020s)** close the loop reflexively: creators hand the whole lineage *back to the audience as theory*, in ninety seconds, on the very algorithmic platforms the theory is now about — the source clip for these notes being a textbook case [35]. The medium diagnosing the condition has become one of the "worlds with terrifying finite edges" it describes.

Two problems fall out of this history. The **accessibility driver** — each stage lowers the barrier to production (label → laptop → screen capture → game engine → phone) — raises the question of whether democratised tooling *spreads* hauntology or *dilutes* it into content. And the **recursion problem**: once the critique of "recycled culture" itself becomes a recognisable, algorithmically-rewarded genre, has it escaped the slow cancellation of the future, or become one more instance of it?

## 7. Where the boundary runs — in-orbit vs. adjacent

Two further works sharpen the concept's edges by splitting cleanly across the two moods. **Severance** (Apple TV+, 2022– ) sits within hauntology's orbit: corporate liminal space, deliberately retro-futurist technology evoking a past's unrealised future of work, and capitalist realism as literal plot [36]. **qntm's** *There Is No Antimemetics Division* (2020) sits on the *eerie* branch instead — absence-plus-agency made systematic (something acts on you that you cannot remember), with a hauntological undertone via Derrida's *trace* (being haunted by holes, by what was erased), but a centre of gravity in cosmic/epistemic horror rather than lost futures [37]. Adjacent screen and page works fill out the map: **Adam Curtis's** *HyperNormalisation* (2016) as a feature-length argument about cultural stasis [38], **David Lynch's** *Twin Peaks: The Return* (2017) [39], **Kyle Edward Ball's** *Skinamarink* (2022) [40], **Mark Z. Danielewski's** *House of Leaves* (2000) [41], and **Jeff VanderMeer's** *Annihilation* (2014) [42]. The diagnostic test is simple: *does the work mourn a cancelled future (hauntology proper) or stage an unexplained absence (the eerie)?* Most things lean one way; a few do both.

## 8. Conclusion

Hauntology is Derrida's pun-turned-diagnosis: the present is never fully itself because it is inhabited by what did not happen. Fisher redirected it from the ghost of Marx to the ghost of the future, arguing that we are haunted not by the past but by the *lost futures* earlier culture imagined and never delivered — a condition produced by the **slow cancellation of the future**, in which technology accelerated while cultural imagination narrowed into endless recycling, the mood of a **capitalist realism** that can no longer picture an alternative. It runs in two registers — hauntology's *lost future* and the *eerie's* absence-and-agency — dramatised respectively by the Backrooms (infrastructures still standing but hollow) and the Farlands (infinities revealed as finite and breaking). And it is not merely a theory but a migrating aesthetic, moving from decayed music through vaporwave and analog horror to liminal-space games and, finally, to the self-aware short-form video essay that folds the whole lineage back onto the very platforms it indicts. Its single deepest claim: what truly haunts us is **the death of the imagination for other ways of being.**
