# Hauntology & Liminal Horror

> "Hauntology is about living among the ghosts of futures that never happened."
> — paraphrasing Mark Fisher, via [@Eterna](https://www.tiktok.com/) ("Liminal horror as our current cultural condition")

Study notes on **hauntology** — the philosophical concept behind why cultural remakes, reboots, and internet myths like *the Backrooms* and *the Farlands* feel quietly unsettling. Each section gives a quick-recall summary, a deeper explanation, and the threads worth following up on.

These notes are built from the short-form video explainer and then cross-checked against the primary sources (Derrida, Fisher, Berardi). Where the video simplifies or compresses, that's flagged so the notes stand on their own.

---

## 1. What Is Hauntology?

**What the idea is testing:** Why our political, cultural, and technological present feels "haunted" — inhabited by things that are neither fully present nor fully absent.

### Quick answer
- **Hauntology** was coined by **Jacques Derrida** in *Spectres of Marx* (1993).
- It's a pun: in French, *hantologie* ("hauntology") sounds almost identical to *ontologie* ("ontology," the study of being).
- The joke has a point: what *haunts* — the spectre, the not-quite-present — unsettles our neat categories of "is" and "is not," "present" and "absent," "living" and "dead."
- Derrida wrote it in the wake of the Soviet Union's collapse, against the triumphant "end of history" mood of the early 1990s.
- The claim: our systems are haunted by **alternatives that never came to be** — futures foreclosed, roads not taken, ghosts that refuse to fully leave.

### Deeper detail

**The historical moment.** By 1993, the Berlin Wall had fallen (1989) and the USSR had dissolved (1991). Francis Fukuyama had famously declared *The End of History* — the argument that liberal capitalism had won the ideological contest for good, and no serious alternative remained. Derrida's *Spectres of Marx* is, in large part, a rebuttal: you cannot simply declare a rival dead and expect it to stay buried. Marx's ghost keeps returning to haunt the very system that pronounced him finished.

**Why "spectre," not just "memory."** A spectre is structurally strange. It is not *present* (it's dead, gone, unrealised) but it is not *absent* either (it acts on us, shapes us, demands something). Derrida's term names exactly this in-between status — being "out of joint," as Hamlet says of his own haunted time. Hauntology is the study of things that persist *as absence*: the influence of what didn't happen.

> **Note on the video's framing.** The clip says hauntology means "our systems were haunted by the alternative ideologies that never came to be." That's a fair one-line gloss, but Derrida's specific target was narrower and sharper: the *specific* ghost of Marx haunting a capitalism that believed it had won. The generalisation to "alternative ideologies" is really Fisher's later expansion (Section 2), not Derrida's original emphasis.

### Threads worth following
- Why is *Hamlet* ("the time is out of joint") Derrida's touchstone text for hauntology?
- How does hauntology relate to Derrida's older idea of the **trace** — presence always shadowed by what it excludes?
- What was Fukuyama's *End of History* actually arguing, and why did Derrida treat it as the thing to refute?

## 2. Fisher's Turn: What Haunts the 21st Century?

**What this is testing:** How a piece of 1990s political philosophy got repurposed to diagnose contemporary culture.

### Quick answer
- **Mark Fisher** (1968–2017), British theorist and critic, took Derrida's term and asked a new question: *what haunts us now?*
- His answer: **not** ideologies, people, or the past — but **the futures we once imagined and never got**.
- We are haunted by our own cancelled futures: the tomorrows that 20th-century culture promised and the 21st century failed to deliver.
- Key works: *Capitalist Realism* (2009) and the essay collection *Ghosts of My Life* (2014).

### Deeper detail

**From politics to culture.** Where Derrida was doing political philosophy, Fisher was a cultural critic — writing about music, film, and design. He noticed that late-20th and early-21st-century culture had developed a strange relationship to time: it kept **recycling its own past** instead of producing anything genuinely new. Vintage aesthetics, endless reissues, sampling, retro revivals. Culture stopped feeling like it was moving *forward*.

**The specific haunting.** For Fisher, the ghost isn't the past as such — nostalgia already covers that (Section 3). The ghost is the **lost future**: the sleek, liberated, collectively imagined tomorrow that mid-century modernity promised (space travel, post-scarcity, radically new art and social forms) and that simply… didn't arrive. What haunts us is the *gap* between the future we were promised and the recycled present we actually inhabit.

**"The death of the imagination for alternative ways of being."** This is the sharpest edge of Fisher's argument, and it ties directly to his other big idea, **capitalist realism**: the widespread sense that capitalism is the only viable system, that "it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism." Hauntology is the *mood* of capitalist realism — the melancholy of a culture that can no longer picture a different way of living, only remixes of what already exists.

### Threads worth following
- What is **capitalist realism**, and how does it explain the "there is no alternative" feeling?
- Fisher wrote extensively about music (Burial, dubstep, "hauntological" artists on the Ghost Box label). Why did *sound* become his central evidence?
- Fisher's own struggles with depression shaped *Ghosts of My Life*. How does he link the personal and the political?

## 3. Nostalgia vs. Hauntology

**What this is testing:** The single most common misreading — collapsing hauntology into "missing the good old days."

### Quick answer
- **Nostalgia** longs for **the past** — a real (or idealised) time that *did* exist.
- **Hauntology** longs for **a future that was imagined in the past and never came to be** — a time that *never* existed.
- Nostalgia wants to *go back*. Hauntology mourns a *forward* that was cancelled.

### Deeper detail

The distinction is the whole game. Nostalgia is comfortable and consumable: it repackages the 1980s, the 1950s, a childhood, and sells it back to you. It points backward at something that happened.

Hauntology points at an **absence in the future tense**. It's the ache of the *Space Age* imagery of the 1960s — not because you want to live in the 1960s, but because that era's *idea of the year 2000* (moon bases, flying cars, a transformed society) was so much bolder than the actual 2000s turned out to be. You aren't missing the past; you're missing the *future the past believed in*.

This is why hauntological art often *sounds* like decayed or half-remembered media — crackle, tape hiss, faded VHS. It's the aesthetic of a signal from a future that got lost in transmission. Nostalgia restores the old image cleanly; hauntology deliberately keeps the damage, because the damage *is* the point.

### Threads worth following
- Why does "retro" marketing sell so easily, while hauntology stays melancholy and hard to commodify?
- Is nostalgia a *symptom* of the same cultural stall Fisher describes, or its opposite?

## 4. The Slow Cancellation of the Future

**What this is testing:** Fisher's core diagnosis of *why* new futures stopped arriving.

### Quick answer
- After the late 20th century, **technology accelerated** but **cultural imagination narrowed**.
- Instead of producing new imagined futures, culture began **recycling old narratives, infrastructures, and expectations**.
- Fisher (borrowing a phrase from **Franco "Bifo" Berardi**) calls this the **"slow cancellation of the future."**

### Deeper detail

**The paradox at the centre.** The gadgets kept improving — faster processors, smartphones, streaming, ubiquitous connectivity. But *culturally*, things flattened. Compare the aesthetic distance between 1960 and 1980 (enormous — whole new genres, sounds, looks) to the distance between 2005 and 2025 (comparatively small). Technology sprinted; imagination idled.

**Why "slow" and why "cancellation."** It isn't a dramatic collapse — no single event killed the future. It's a *gradual* attrition: a reboot here, a legacy sequel there, a revival, a remaster, until you look up and realise the culture is almost entirely feeding on itself. The future wasn't destroyed; it was quietly *cancelled*, the way a TV show gets cancelled — the sets are still standing, but no new episodes are being made.

> **Attribution note.** The phrase "slow cancellation of the future" is often credited to Fisher, and he did the most to popularise it, but he took it from the Italian theorist **Franco "Bifo" Berardi**. Worth crediting both — the video attributes it to Fisher alone.

**Remakes and reboots as evidence.** This is where the concept bites into everyday experience. The unease you feel at yet another franchise reboot, live-action remake, or "requel" isn't just taste. It's a small, felt instance of the slow cancellation: culture demonstrating, in real time, that it would rather re-run a known quantity than risk imagining something new.

### Threads worth following
- Is the "stall" real, or a selection effect (we remember the innovations of the past and forget its filler)?
- How do economics — risk-averse studios, franchise IP, algorithmic recommendation — *materially* drive the recycling Fisher describes?

## 5. Case Study: The Backrooms — Empty Promises

**What this is testing:** How an internet horror myth *stages* hauntology as empty space.

### Quick answer
- The **Backrooms** is a creepypasta / liminal-space myth: endless empty **corporate interiors** — yellowed wallpaper, buzzing **fluorescent lights**, familiar office and retail architecture, **devastatingly empty**.
- Through Fisher's lens: these spaces symbolise the **infrastructures of 21st-century life** — corporate prosperity, stable careers, endless economic growth, modern efficiency.
- The horror is that **we still live inside these infrastructures, but the futures they promised never arrived.** The spaces remain; the promise is gone.

### Deeper detail

**Origin.** The Backrooms began around 2019 as a single unsettling photo and caption on 4chan, and snowballed into a vast collaborative mythology (later a much-shared found-footage series by Kane Pixels). The defining trait is the **liminal space**: architecture designed for *transit and commerce* — hallways, waiting rooms, office floors, mall corridors — encountered *empty*, at the wrong hour, drained of the people and purpose that were supposed to fill it.

**Why it's hauntological, not just creepy.** These aren't ruins or haunted houses. They're the *ordinary* environments of late capitalism — the office, the retail floor, the corporate campus. Each was built as a machine for delivering a future: the career, the growth, the efficiency, the prosperity. The Backrooms show you that machinery *still standing and still lit*, but **hollow** — the human future it was supposed to produce has evaporated. That's the exact structure of Fisher's ghost: the promise persists as an empty shell you're still trapped inside.

> "The spaces remain, but the futures that they promised are devastatingly empty."

### Threads worth following
- Why do *liminal spaces* specifically (thresholds, in-between places) carry this charge? Connect to the anthropology of liminality (Victor Turner).
- Backrooms vs. **Weird / Eerie** — Fisher's own late book *The Weird and the Eerie* (2016) offers a vocabulary: the *eerie* is precisely about **absence and agency** ("why is there nothing here when there should be something?").

## 6. Case Study: The Farlands — Broken Edges

**What this is testing:** A second, contrasting face of the same haunting — not emptiness, but the *edge* of the imaginable.

### Quick answer
- The **Farlands** originated as a **Minecraft** phenomenon: push far enough toward the edges of the procedurally generated world and the terrain **breaks** — walls of distorted, glitched geometry where the generation algorithm fails.
- The metaphor migrated to **TikTok**: pushing your *algorithm* toward its strangest, most niche edges until the recommendation engine's "rules begin to break."
- Through Fisher's lens: the Farlands aren't *empty* like the Backrooms — they're **broken**. The horror is the **death of the imagination**: worlds that felt *infinite* turn out to have **terrifyingly finite edges**.

### Deeper detail

**Origin (the real bug).** In early Minecraft, world generation relied on floating-point math that lost precision at extreme coordinates (around 12.5 million blocks from spawn). The result was the "Far Lands": a chaotic wall of malformed terrain marking the literal edge of the computable world. It was a *bug*, but a poetic one — proof that the "infinite" sandbox had a boundary where its own logic disintegrated.

**The TikTok translation.** Online culture repurposed the term. To reach "the Farlands" of TikTok is to push the recommendation algorithm — through niche, recursive, increasingly strange engagement — until the feed stops feeling curated and starts feeling *malfunctioning*, surfacing content from the weird outer edge of the system. Same shape as the Minecraft bug: keep going toward the boundary and the generating system starts to break down.

**Why it's hauntological.** The Backrooms haunt through *empty promise*; the Farlands haunt through *broken infinity*. Our algorithmic worlds — the feed, the platform, the endlessly scrolling stream — *present themselves as boundless*, an inexhaustible supply of the new. The Farlands reveal the lie: there is an edge, and at the edge the machinery just produces glitched repetitions of itself. That's the "death of the imagination" made literal — the infinite generator running out of genuinely new things to generate, and breaking.

### Threads worth following
- Backrooms = the **eerie** of *absence* (nothing where there should be something); Farlands = the **eerie** of *malfunction* (the system failing at its own edge). Do they map onto two different Fisherian moods?
- How does the *feeling* of an "infinite feed" with a hidden finite edge connect back to the slow cancellation — abundance of content, scarcity of the genuinely new?

## 7. Synthesis: Liminal Horror as Our Cultural Condition

**What this is testing:** Pulling the strands together into a single claim.

### Quick answer
- For Fisher, **hauntology is living among the ghosts of futures that never happened.**
- The **Backrooms** (empty) and the **Farlands** (broken) both depict worlds where **past expectations of the future** and **current lived reality** are **fractured**.
- We can't **metabolise** the difference between what was promised and what is — so we're **trapped in an echo of everything we could have been.**

### Deeper detail

The two myths are complementary diagnoses of one condition:

| | **The Backrooms** | **The Farlands** |
|---|---|---|
| Core image | Empty corporate interiors | Broken edges of a generated world |
| Failure mode | The promise is *hollow* | The infinity is *finite* |
| Haunted by | Futures that were promised but never filled | The end of the imagination's frontier |
| Fisher's mood | The **eerie** of absence | The **eerie** of malfunction |
| Everyday echo | Careers, growth, "prosperity" that didn't materialise | Feeds/algorithms that seemed endless, then hit their limit |

Both are **liminal**: thresholds, in-between zones, edges. And that's the point of calling liminal horror our *current cultural condition*. We are, collectively, standing in a doorway — inside infrastructures built for a future that never came, scrolling worlds that promised infinity and delivered a loop. The horror isn't a monster. It's the quiet recognition, in a fluorescent-lit corridor or at the glitched edge of a feed, that the future got cancelled while we were still living in its architecture.

### Threads worth following
- Is liminal horror a genuinely *new* structure of feeling, or the latest costume for older anxieties (the uncanny, the sublime, *fin-de-siècle* dread)?
- If Fisher is right that the imagination has stalled, what would *un-cancelling* the future even look like? (Fisher's later, more hopeful "acid communism" fragment gestures at this.)
- Counter-argument: is the "lost future" thesis itself a kind of nostalgia — mourning a mid-century modernism that was never as universal or as liberating as it's remembered?

## 8. How the Domain Has Progressed: A Media Lineage

**What this is testing:** Hauntology and liminal horror aren't just a theory — they're a living aesthetic that has *moved through media* over ~25 years, mutating each time it changed platforms. Tracing that progression shows how a piece of academic philosophy became a folk-internet genre and, finally, its own self-aware discourse (the explainer video that started these notes).

### Quick answer
- **2000s — Music.** Hauntology is *first* an art movement, mostly in electronic music. This is the material Fisher actually wrote about.
- **~2010 — Internet aesthetics.** Vaporwave translates the mood into a native-online, image-and-sound meme about dead consumer futures.
- **Mid–late 2010s — Analog horror (YouTube).** Degraded-broadcast storytelling turns the *aesthetic of decayed media* into a horror grammar.
- **2020s — Liminal-space myths & games.** The Backrooms, the Farlands, and a wave of walking-sim games make the *space itself* the subject.
- **2020s–now — Short-form video essays.** Creators like @Eterna metabolise the whole lineage back into theory for a TikTok audience — the domain becomes self-aware.

Each stage doesn't replace the last; it *layers* on top, and the tools get more accessible each time (studio → bedroom producer → YouTube channel → game engine → phone).

### The lineage in detail

**Stage 1 — Music: the original hauntological canon (roughly 1998–2011).**
This is the ground truth, because it's the media Fisher himself analysed.
- **Burial** — *Untrue* (2007, Hyperdub). Fisher's central exhibit: crackle, vinyl surface noise, and pitched, ghostly vocals that sound like a rave remembered rather than attended — the lost collective euphoria of '90s UK dance music.
- **Ghost Box** (label, founded 2004) — **Belbury Poly**, **The Focus Group**, **Pye Corner Audio**. They fabricate a "parallel England" out of library music, public-information films, and mid-century BBC textures — mourning the *postwar social-democratic future* that Britain abandoned.
- **The Caretaker** (Leyland Kirby) — *An Empty Bliss Beyond This World* (2011) and the six-stage *Everywhere at the End of Time* (2016–2019), which musically enacts the progression of dementia through decaying ballroom loops. Notably, this *pre-internet-native* work went massively viral on **TikTok/YouTube around 2020** as a memory challenge — an early sign of the lineage folding back on itself.
- **William Basinski** — *The Disintegration Loops* (2002): tape literally crumbling as it plays, recorded against the backdrop of 9/11. Decay as content.
- **Boards of Canada** — *Music Has the Right to Children* (1998): warped, sun-faded analog nostalgia that predates and prefigures the whole mood.

**Stage 2 — Vaporwave and the internet-native turn (~2010–2015).**
Hauntology leaves the record label and becomes a *meme* anyone with a laptop can make.
- **Chuck Person's *Eccojams Vol. 1*** (Daniel Lopatin / Oneohtrix Point Never, 2010) and **Macintosh Plus — *Floral Shoppe*** (Vektroid, 2011) are the touchstones: slowed, chopped '80s–'90s corporate pop, muzak, and smooth jazz, wrapped in Greco-Roman busts and dead-mall imagery.
- The point is explicitly hauntological: vaporwave mourns the *utopia that consumer capitalism promised* — the gleaming, frictionless techno-future of shopping malls, Windows 95, and corporate optimism — by exhuming its muzak and letting it rot. Subgenres like **mallsoft** and **signalwave** make the "devastatingly empty commercial interior" (see the Backrooms, Section 5) into a *sound* years before it was a horror image.

**Stage 3 — Analog horror on YouTube (~2015–present).**
The decayed-media *aesthetic* from Stages 1–2 becomes a full horror storytelling grammar.
- **Local58** (Kris Straub, 2015), **Gemini Home Entertainment** (2019), **The Mandela Catalogue** (Alex Kister, 2021), **The Walten Files** (2020). Also the ARG **Petscop** (2017).
- The shared move: tell horror through the *format of obsolete media* — VHS tracking errors, emergency-broadcast interruptions, corrupted PSA reels, glitched PS1 games. The **medium is the haunting**: a signal from a past that imagined a safe, ordered future (public broadcasting, children's edutainment) now returning *corrupted*. This is hauntology as genre convention, not just mood.

**Stage 4 — Liminal-space myths and games (2019–present).**
Now the *space itself* is the subject — the stage these notes' two case studies belong to.
- Folklore: **The Backrooms** (2019 →), supercharged by **Kane Pixels'** cinematic found-footage series (2022, an A24 film in development); the **Farlands** (Minecraft's floating-point edge, then a TikTok metaphor).
- Games make it interactive: **The Exit 8** (Kotake Create, 2023 — an endless, looping subway passage you escape only by noticing anomalies), **Pools** (2024), **Anemoiapolis**, **The Complex: Found Footage** (2022). Adjacent AAA: **Control** (Remedy, 2019) and its brutalist "Oldest House" as a study in the Fisherian *eerie*.
- Prestige screen work runs in parallel: **Skinamarink** (2022) for domestic analog dread; **Twin Peaks: The Return** (2017) and **Adam Curtis's *HyperNormalisation*** (2016) as feature-length arguments about cultural stasis.

**Stage 5 — The video-essay meta-turn (2020s–now).**
The final progression is *reflexive*. Creators on TikTok and YouTube — the @Eterna clip these notes come from is a textbook example — take the whole 25-year lineage and hand it *back to the audience as theory*, in 60–180 seconds, on the very platforms (TikTok, algorithmic feeds) that the theory is now about. The domain has become self-aware: the medium diagnosing the condition is *itself* one of the "algorithmic worlds with terrifying finite edges" it describes.

### Media map

| Stage | Era | Medium | Exemplars | What it haunts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1998–2011 | Electronic music | Burial, Ghost Box, The Caretaker, Basinski, Boards of Canada | Lost postwar/rave futures; decay of memory |
| 2 | 2010–2015 | Internet music + image | Macintosh Plus, Oneohtrix Point Never, mallsoft | The dead utopia of consumer capitalism |
| 3 | 2015–now | YouTube analog horror | Local58, Mandela Catalogue, Gemini Home Ent., Petscop | Corrupted broadcast/edutainment futures |
| 4 | 2019–now | Folklore + games + film | Backrooms, Farlands, The Exit 8, Control, Skinamarink | Empty/broken infrastructures and worlds |
| 5 | 2020s–now | Short-form video essay | @Eterna and peers | The feed diagnosing itself |

### Threads worth following
- **Accessibility as the driver.** Each stage lowers the barrier to production (label → DAW → screen-capture → game engine → phone). Does democratised tooling *spread* hauntology, or dilute it into content?
- **The recursion problem.** When the critique of "recycled culture" itself becomes a recognisable, algorithmically-rewarded content genre, has it escaped the slow cancellation or become another instance of it?
- **The Caretaker on TikTok** is the sharpest case: a 2010s art project about memory-loss going viral as a 2020s challenge. Is that the lineage healing, or eating itself?
- Where does **AI-generated media** — infinite, uncanny, edge-less until it suddenly glitches — fit as a possible Stage 6?

## Glossary

- **Hauntology** — Derrida's pun on *ontology*; the study of how the not-quite-present (spectres, lost futures, cancelled alternatives) acts on the present.
- **Spectre** — that which is neither present nor absent, neither living nor dead; the figure at the heart of hauntology.
- **Capitalist realism** (Fisher) — the pervasive sense that capitalism is the only viable system and no alternative can be imagined.
- **Slow cancellation of the future** (Berardi, via Fisher) — the gradual replacement of new cultural production with recycled versions of the past.
- **Lost / cancelled futures** — the tomorrows imagined by past culture that never materialised; hauntology's primary object of mourning.
- **The eerie** (Fisher) — a mood built on *absence* or *unexplained agency*: "why is there nothing here?" / "what is acting here?" (contrast with **the weird**, built on the presence of what *shouldn't* be there).
- **Liminal space** — an in-between, transitional environment (corridors, waiting rooms, thresholds), unsettling when encountered empty or out of context.
- **The Backrooms** — internet horror myth of endless empty liminal interiors (c. 2019–).
- **The Farlands** — the glitched edge of Minecraft's generated world (a floating-point bug), later a metaphor for pushing an algorithmic feed to its breaking point.
