Essay · Cultural Theory

Hauntology:
An Account

A single continuous argument — the concept as we developed it, from Derrida’s coinage to the self-aware video essay. Claims are cited to the numbered Sources below.

§ 01

The Origin — A Pun With a Point

Hauntology begins as wordplay and ends as a diagnosis of an era. Jacques Derrida coined it in Spectres de Marx (1993; English Specters of Marx, 1994)1, building it on a near-homophone: in French, hantologie (“hauntology”) is almost indistinguishable in speech from ontologie (“ontology,” the philosophy of being). The pun is load-bearing. Ontology asks what is — what is present, real, fully itself. Hauntology names everything that troubles that question: the spectre, which is neither present nor absent, neither living nor dead, yet acts on the living all the same1.

The historical occasion mattered. By 1993 the Berlin Wall had fallen and the Soviet Union had dissolved, and Francis Fukuyama had announced The End of History — liberal capitalism had won permanently; no serious alternative remained2. Derrida’s book is largely a refusal of that triumphalism: you cannot pronounce a rival dead and expect it to stay buried, and Marx’s ghost keeps returning to haunt the very order that declared him finished1. A spectre is what persists as absence — the continuing pressure of something unrealised or refused. To be haunted is to live in a time that is, in the line from Hamlet Derrida keeps returning to, “out of joint.”

The concept also draws on Derrida’s older idea of the trace — the claim, from Of Grammatology (1967), that presence is always constituted by what it excludes3 — and sits in a long lineage of thought about the unhomely, reaching back to Freud’s uncanny (das Unheimliche, 1919)4. The seed of all of it: the present is never fully itself, because it is inhabited by what did not happen.

§ 02

Fisher’s Turn — From the Ghost of Marx to the Ghost of the Future

The concept as we have used it is really the work of Mark Fisher (1968–2017), who pointed Derrida’s term at contemporary culture567. The move had been prepared in mid-2000s music criticism — Fisher (writing as k-punk) and Simon Reynolds developed a musical sense of “hauntology” on their blogs before it reached print89 — but Fisher gave it mature form in Ghosts of My Life (2014)6 and the essay “What Is Hauntology?” (2012)10.

Fisher asked: if we are haunted now, what haunts us? His answer is the pivot. We are not primarily haunted by ideologies, by the dead, or by the past as such. We are haunted by the futures we once imagined and never got — the lost tomorrows earlier culture promised and the present quietly failed to deliver610. The mid-century dream of a transformed society doesn’t merely fail to arrive; it lingers, as an ache that we are living in an impoverished version of a future that should have been bolder.

Nostalgia longs for the past — a time that did exist. Hauntology longs for a future imagined in the past that never came to be.

Nostalgia is comfortable and saleable; it repackages the eighties and sells it back to you. Fredric Jameson had already diagnosed this “nostalgia mode” — pastiche, the imitation of dead styles — as a symptom of late capitalism11, and hauntology is its sharper, sadder sibling. It points at an absence in the future tense — not “I miss the 1960s” but “I miss the year 2000 the 1960s believed in.” This is why hauntological art tends to sound and look damaged — tape hiss, crackle, faded VHS610. The damage is the meaning: a signal from a promised future lost in transmission.

§ 03

The Mechanism — The Slow Cancellation of the Future

Fisher’s explanation for why those futures went missing is his sharpest claim, and he named it — borrowing the phrase from Franco “Bifo” Berardi12 — the slow cancellation of the future6.

The engine is a paradox. Across the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, technology accelerated — processors, smartphones, streaming — while cultural imagination narrowed. Measure the aesthetic distance from 1960 to 1980 (enormous) against 2005 to 2025 (comparatively slight), and the stall is visible. The machines sprinted; the imagination idled. Instead of new futures, culture began recycling its own past — a condition Simon Reynolds anatomised as Retromania (2011)9.

“Cancellation” and “slow” are both chosen precisely. No single event killed the future; it was gradual attrition — a legacy sequel, a live-action remake — until the culture is feeding on itself. The future was cancelled the way a television show is cancelled: the sets still stand, still lit, but no new episodes are made. Underneath sits capitalist realism: the sense that capitalism is the only viable system, that “it is easier to imagine the end of the world than the end of capitalism”5. Hauntology is its mood — a culture that can no longer picture an alternative, only remixes. What haunts us is the death of the imagination for alternative futures.

§ 04

Two Moods — Hauntology and the Eerie

A second distinction becomes essential — one Fisher draws in The Weird and the Eerie (2016)7. Alongside hauntology’s temporal register (lost futures) is the eerie, built on absence and unexplained agency: why is there nothing here where there should be something? what is acting here that I cannot see? He distinguishes it from the weird, the intrusion of something that should not be present7. These registers — lost future, eerie absence, weird intrusion — are the branches everything else sorts onto.

The concept also leans on an older anthropological term: liminality, from Arnold van Gennep’s Rites of Passage (1909) and Victor Turner’s elaboration of the “betwixt and between” threshold state1314. A liminal space is transitional by design — a corridor, a waiting room — and becomes unsettling when encountered empty or out of use; the phenomenology of such places has itself been theorised (Dylan Trigg, The Memory of Place, 2012)15.

§ 05

The Two Internet Myths

The two flagship myths dramatise the two moods. The Backrooms — from a 2019 image-and-caption post on 4chan’s /x/ board16, given cinematic form by Kane Pixels’ The Backrooms (Found Footage) (2022)17 — stage hauntology as empty space: endless liminal corporate interiors, yellowed wallpaper, buzzing fluorescents, devastatingly empty. Read through Fisher, they are the infrastructures of 21st-century life — career, growth, prosperity, efficiency — still standing and still lit, but hollow. The spaces remain; the promise is gone. (The kindred SCP entry SCP-3008, the “infinite IKEA,” is the same intuition in collaborative-fiction form18.)

The Farlands stage the same haunting as broken infinity. The name comes from a real Minecraft bug: at ~12.5 million blocks from spawn, floating-point imprecision corrupts world generation into a wall of malformed geometry — the edge of the computable world, present until it was patched out in Beta 1.8 (2011)19. Transposed to TikTok, “reaching the Farlands” means pushing the recommendation algorithm through niche, recursive engagement until the feed feels malfunctioning. Where the Backrooms are empty, the Farlands are broken: worlds that present themselves as boundless, revealed to have terrifyingly finite edges where the generator runs out of new things to make and glitches out repetitions. The death of the imagination, made literal.

Both are liminal — thresholds, edges. We are collectively standing in a doorway: inside infrastructures built for a future that never came, scrolling worlds that promised infinity and delivered a loop. The horror isn’t a monster; it’s the recognition, in a fluorescent corridor or at the glitched edge of a feed, that the future got cancelled while we were still living in its architecture.

§ 06

The Domain as a Living Lineage

Hauntology is not only a theory but a living aesthetic that has migrated across media for a quarter-century, becoming more accessible at every step.

  • Music (c. 1998–2011) — the material Fisher analysed: Boards of Canada’s Music Has the Right to Children (1998)20; Burial’s Untrue (2007)21; the Ghost Box label’s “parallel England”22; The Caretaker’s Everywhere at the End of Time (2016–2019)23; William Basinski’s Disintegration Loops (2002–2003)24.
  • Vaporwave (c. 2010–2015) — Chuck Person’s Eccojams Vol. 1 (2010)25 and Macintosh Plus’s Floral Shoppe (2011)26 mourning the utopia consumer capitalism promised. It made the empty commercial interior a sound before it was a horror image.
  • Analog horror on YouTube (c. 2015–now) — Local58 (2015)27, Petscop (2017)28, Gemini Home Entertainment (2019)29, The Walten Files (2020)30, The Mandela Catalogue (2021)31: the medium itself becomes the haunting.
  • Liminal-space myths & games (2019–now) — the Backrooms and Farlands, plus Control (2019)32, The Exit 8 (2023)33, Pools (2024)34.
  • Short-form video essays (2020s) close the loop reflexively, handing the lineage back as theory on the very platforms it is now about35.

Two problems fall out: the accessibility driver (each stage lowers the barrier — label → laptop → screen capture → game engine → phone) asks whether tooling spreads hauntology or dilutes it; and the recursion problem — once the critique of recycled culture becomes an algorithmically-rewarded genre, has it escaped the slow cancellation or become one more instance of it?

§ 07

Where the Boundary Runs

Two further works sharpen the concept by splitting across the two moods. Severance (Apple TV+, 2022– ) sits within hauntology’s orbit: corporate liminal space, retro-futurist technology evoking a past’s unrealised future of work, capitalist realism as literal plot36. qntm’s There Is No Antimemetics Division (2020) sits on the eerie branch — absence-plus-agency made systematic (something acts on you that you cannot remember), with a hauntological undertone via the trace, but a centre of gravity in cosmic/epistemic horror37. Adjacent works fill the map: Adam Curtis’s HyperNormalisation (2016)38, Lynch’s Twin Peaks: The Return (2017)39, Ball’s Skinamarink (2022)40, Danielewski’s House of Leaves (2000)41, VanderMeer’s Annihilation (2014)42. The test: does the work mourn a cancelled future (hauntology) or stage an unexplained absence (the eerie)?

§ 08 · Conclusion

The Death of the Imagination for Other Ways of Being

Hauntology is Derrida’s pun-turned-diagnosis: the present is never fully itself because it is inhabited by what did not happen. Fisher redirected it from the ghost of Marx to the ghost of the future — we are haunted by the lost futures earlier culture imagined and never delivered, a condition produced by the slow cancellation of the future, the mood of a capitalist realism that can no longer picture an alternative. It runs in two registers — the lost future and the eerie’s absence-and-agency — dramatised by the Backrooms (infrastructures still standing but hollow) and the Farlands (infinities revealed as finite and breaking). And it is a migrating aesthetic, moving from decayed music through vaporwave and analog horror to liminal-space games and, finally, to the self-aware video essay that folds the whole lineage back onto the platforms it indicts. Its deepest claim: what truly haunts us is the death of the imagination for other ways of being.
